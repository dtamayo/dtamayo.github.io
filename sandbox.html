The Driven Pendulum: Surface of Section

Keep only the state $(\theta, v)$ at $t = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, once per drive period. Because the equations repeat every period, the state at $t = n$ completely determines the state at $t = n+1$, so this is a well-defined, area-preserving map of the plane to itself. Click anywhere to launch an orbit and plot its crossings. Then read the plot:

  • Smooth curve: a regular orbit, a slice through an invariant torus. Some approximate conserved quantity survived, even though energy didn't.
  • Closed loops around a point: a resonance island. Compare its size with the shaded band (the pendulum estimate $2\sqrt{\alpha}$ or $2\sqrt{\epsilon}$).
  • Chains of small islands (e.g. near $v \approx \pm 3$): secondary resonances, created by the interaction of the primary waves.
  • Scattered cloud filling an area: a chaotic orbit. Chaos appears first near separatrices.

Populate launches a grid of orbits for a quick overview. Changing $\alpha$ or $\epsilon$ clears the plot.

Click on the plot to launch an orbit.

The Driven Pendulum: Sensitive Dependence on Initial Conditions

The surface of section tells regular and chaotic orbits apart by their shape: curves versus scattered clouds. The other defining property of chaos is sensitive dependence on initial conditions. Click the left panel to launch two orbits that start a tiny distance $d_0$ apart (they differ only in $\theta_0$). On the left, orbit A is solid with a filled dot and orbit B is dashed with an open dot. On the right, the distance between them in phase space, $d(t) = \sqrt{\Delta\theta^2 + \Delta v^2}$ (with $\Delta\theta$ taken modulo $2\pi$), is traced out in sync.

  • Regular orbit (on a curve or inside an island): the two orbits drift apart only slowly, roughly linearly in time, so on a linear scale $d(t)$ stays glued to zero.
  • Chaotic orbit: $d(t)$ looks flat for a while, then explodes, growing like $e^{\lambda t}$ until the orbits are as far apart as the chaotic region allows.

Try $\theta_0 = \pi$, $v_0 \approx 0.5$ (in the chaotic layer near the unstable fixed point) against $\theta_0 = 0.8$, $v_0 = 0$ (deep in the central island). Each new click keeps earlier distance curves, faded, for comparison. Tick log scale to see that chaotic growth is a straight line, whose slope is the Lyapunov exponent $\lambda$.

The Three-Body Problem: Namouni Map and N-body

Visualization by Aron Zhou.

A planet (white) on a circular orbit at 1 AU, with $3\times10^{-6}$ of its star's mass (about the Earth's), and a massless test body on a slightly larger orbit near the 10:9 resonance. The left panel is a surface of section from the Namouni map, which treats each conjunction with the planet as a kick to the test body's orbit. Click it to start an orbit there. The same click starts an N-body integration from the same initial condition, shown in the middle panel in a frame that rotates with the test body's pericentre, so the pericentre always points up. A line from the star marks the direction of each conjunction. The right panel magnifies the dashed box, with faint traces of the test body's earlier passes, so the change in its orbit at each conjunction shows up as a shift between groups of passes.
